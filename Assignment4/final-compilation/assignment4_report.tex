\SectionBar{1. Problem Setup, Data Pipeline, and Model Design}
{\fontsize{7.55}{9.55}\selectfont\justifying
The task is to replicate the S\&P 500 daily return profile with a sparse long-only portfolio under the cardinality constraint $k \leq 50$. After validating the raw OHLCV files, we retained \ReadableFiles{} readable stock histories, excluded 6 issue files (5 empty CSVs and one non-price cache file), removed 24 late entrants / early exits, and dropped one additional ticker (CVG) because it still contained 25 missing training prices. The final model-ready matrix covers \texttt{2020-01-03} to \texttt{2025-12-31} and contains 553 stocks plus the benchmark. Validation and holdout price holes were forward-filled before return construction, while the training block was kept hole-free by design.

\vspace{3pt}
The cardinality-constrained tracking problem is an \textbf{MIQP} and is therefore \textbf{NP-hard}. To keep the comparison fair, all methods use the same two-stage pipeline: \textbf{(i)} method-specific stock selection and \textbf{(ii)} a shared long-only quadratic program that minimizes the variance of active returns subject to $\sum_i w_i = 1$ and $w_i \ge 0$. We report annualized \textbf{Tracking Error (TE)} and \textbf{Information Ratio (IR)}, where \textbf{TE} should be low and \textbf{IR} should stay close to zero for a true index tracker.
}

  \textbf{Autoencoder} & Symmetric \textbf{autoencoder} (\texttt{553 $\rightarrow$ 128 $\rightarrow$ 32 $\rightarrow$ 128 $\rightarrow$ 553}); stocks ranked by communality (reconstruction $R^2$) and top-$k$ retained. & Captures shared latent structure, but unsupervised ranking can over-concentrate in sectors with strong common factor loadings. \\
  \textbf{Genetic Algorithm} & Population search over $k$-stock subsets; fitness equals negative training \textbf{TE}, with crossover, mutation, and shared QP reweighting. & Computationally heavier, but best at directly optimizing sparse subset quality under the replication objective. \\

The tracking error vs sparsity curve shows the expected \textbf{diminishing-return pattern}: all methods improve rapidly from $k=10$ to around $k=50$, after which \textbf{TE} reductions become incremental. \textbf{Lasso} is especially brittle at small $k$ (\textbf{TE} above 25\% at $k \approx 10$), while \textbf{GA} already remains below 8\% at $k=10$, indicating better subset quality under severe sparsity. The \textbf{IR} plot highlights that low \textbf{TE} alone is not sufficient: \textbf{Lasso} and \textbf{Autoencoder} both exhibit unstable directional bets across validation and holdout, whereas \textbf{GA}'s holdout \textbf{IR} of -0.10 is the clearest evidence of \textbf{genuine replication} rather than accidental active management.

\SectionBar{6. Conclusion}
{\fontsize{7.55}{9.55}\selectfont\justifying
Across all methods, the \textbf{Genetic Algorithm} turned out to be a good replication method. It achieves the lowest validation and holdout \textbf{TE} at the target of $k=50$, keeps holdout \textbf{IR} closest to zero, and exhibits the cleanest \textbf{sector alignment} with the benchmark. \textbf{Lasso} remains attractive for speed and interpretability but is vulnerable to regime-specific tilts, while the \textbf{Autoencoder}'s unsupervised selection objective produces excessive \textbf{sector concentration}. The key empirical takeaway is that, for \textbf{sparse index replication}, directly optimizing subset quality with a search-based method and then reweighting through a common long-only \textbf{QP} yields a \textbf{stable out-of-sample tracker}.
}
