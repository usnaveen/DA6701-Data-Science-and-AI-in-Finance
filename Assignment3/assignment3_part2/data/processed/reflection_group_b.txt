
REFLECTION — Group B: Structural Regularization via HRP
========================================================

Hierarchical Risk Parity (HRP) represents a fundamentally different form of 
regularization compared to Ledoit-Wolf (LW) shrinkage. Whereas LW operates 
numerically on the covariance matrix — shrinking extreme eigenvalues toward a 
structured target to reduce estimation noise — HRP regularizes structurally by 
respecting the topology of risk relationships uncovered through hierarchical clustering.

Using single-linkage clustering on a correlation-based distance metric 
d = √(0.5(1 − ρ)), we extracted a hierarchical representation of co-movement 
patterns in our 12-stock NSE universe. The resulting dendrogram revealed clear 
and economically intuitive groupings: commodity-linked cyclicals (metals), 
rate-sensitive financials (private banks), export-oriented technology firms, 
and domestic consumption defensives. Importantly, these clusters emerged purely 
from return co-movement — no sector labels were supplied.

The quasi-diagonalized covariance matrix further validates this structure. 
High covariance values concentrate within blocks corresponding to these clusters, 
while cross-cluster covariances are comparatively weaker. Notably, metals and 
banks exhibit positive cross-correlation due to shared pro-cyclical exposure, 
yet intra-sector covariance remains stronger than inter-sector covariance. 
This indicates the presence of both macroeconomic and sector-specific risk factors 
and confirms that the clustering captures genuine hierarchical market structure.

The practical implication is substantial. Standard Mean-Variance Optimization (MVO) 
treats all pairwise correlations as equally reliable inputs, often resulting in 
unstable and highly concentrated portfolios driven by estimation noise. HRP, by 
contrast, allocates risk recursively: first across structurally distinct clusters, 
then within clusters in inverse proportion to cluster variance. This multi-scale 
allocation framework reduces sensitivity to small perturbations in correlation 
estimates.

Our turnover analysis reinforces this conclusion. HRP portfolios exhibit more stable 
weights and require less aggressive rebalancing than unconstrained MVO. The 
hierarchical scaffold acts as a structural regularizer: small estimation errors do 
not cascade into large weight swings because allocation decisions are made at the 
cluster level before reaching the asset level.

In high-dimensional financial settings, raw sample covariance matrices are 
notoriously unstable. LW shrinkage improves numerical conditioning, but HRP 
improves structural robustness. Rather than trusting a single global covariance 
estimate, HRP builds portfolios on discovered risk hierarchies — producing 
allocations that are more interpretable, economically grounded, and resilient 
out-of-sample.
